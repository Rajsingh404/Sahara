SAHARA · wristband concept

A sound you can feel on your wrist

Turn your volume up: each sound plays first, then the phone alerts and you hear the band's motor hum in its pattern. Sounds are synthesised in the browser, so they are stand-ins for the real thing.

The phone listens. When it hears a doorbell, a smoke alarm or someone calling Aryan, it sends four bytes over Bluetooth, and the band on the wrist buzzes a pattern that tells the sound apart without looking at a screen.

Concept.The band is designed but not yet built. Its firmware compiles for the ESP32 and the phone app already sends the alerts. The motor, battery and enclosure have not been assembled or measured.

From sound to buzz

What happens in the second after the doorbell rings

Everything up to step 4 runs today on a Samsung Galaxy S24. Steps 5 and 6 are the band.

  1. HearThe phone keeps the last second of 16 kHz audio in a RAM ring buffer. Nothing is written to disk and the release app has no internet permission.
  2. RecogniseYAMNet turns each window into an embedding; a small trained head scores the 8 sounds. A separate spotter listens for the name "Aryan" and checks the caller's voice against enrolled family members.
  3. DecideEach sound has its own threshold and needs 1 or 2 of the last 3 windows, tuned to about 4 false alarms an hour. A 10 s cooldown stops repeats.
  4. SendThe app writes 4 bytes to the band: version, command, class id, strength. Safety sounds always go at full strength (255); others at the model's confidence, never below 128.
  5. BuzzThe band plays that sound's pattern on a coin motor and blinks its LED. A newer alert interrupts the one playing; the button stops it.
  6. Stay quiet otherwiseBetween alerts the band idles at 80 MHz with low radio power, checks its battery every 30 s and tells the wearer with two long buzzes if the phone disconnects.

Wiring

One cell, one motor, one board

The motor runs straight from the cell through the MOSFET, so its current never sags the 3.3 V rail. A 20 kHz PWM drives it above the range of hearing. A resistor divider lets the board read its own battery.
